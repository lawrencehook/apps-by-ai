Jeongganbo 정간보

Korean musicians began writing music into a measured grid at the court of King Sejong in the fifteenth century. Reading starts at the top right. Each column runs down the page, and the next begins to its left.

Cheongsanbyeolgok 청산별곡

Anonymous · Goryeo song · Seven columns · Two-page opening

Each group in this opening has a melody column, percussion cues and words. A character in the melody column gives a new pitch. Empty cells let it continue.

Select a cell to hear the phrase from that point. Read each column downwards, moving left at the end of the cycle.

Score and sound

In Cheongsanbyeolgok, the woodblock score preserves the melody in pyeongjo mode. The character 宮 marks its central pitch, while 上 and 下 count scale steps above and below it. The player sounds the melody alone, using an even pulse, G as its central pitch and a synthesized tone. National Gugak Center on pitch notation

The 112 cells were transcribed directly from this public-domain image of Siyong hyangakbo. Facsimile record

The four digital editions come from the Jeonggan editor’s example scores. Their melody is played on sampled piano, accompanied by recorded strokes of the janggu, a Korean hourglass drum. The grid follows the edition’s pitch characters, subdivisions, and ornamental signs. Source editions
